## Why this matters in a job

Because on day one you will be added to six group chats and will not know who
any of these people are or what they are allowed to ask you for. Knowing the map
is the difference between "the new person is settling in fast" and "the new
person keeps escalating things to the wrong team".

## The teams

::field-group
  ::field{name="Engineering" type="builds it"}
  Writes and maintains the code. Split into frontend (what the user sees),
  backend (the logic and data behind it), mobile, and sometimes platform. This is
  the job this curriculum is preparing you for.
  ::

  ::field{name="QA / Quality" type="proves it works"}
  Designs test cases, runs them, automates them, and finds the thing you did not
  think of. In many companies QA is where the deepest knowledge of what the
  product actually does lives.
  ::

  ::field{name="DevOps / SRE / Platform" type="runs it"}
  Owns the pipelines, servers, deployments and monitoring. When the site is down
  at 2am, this is who is awake. Increasingly a first-job path of its own.
  ::

  ::field{name="Product" type="decides what to build"}
  Talks to users and the business, decides what is worth building next, writes it
  down as requirements, and answers your questions about edge cases. Your closest
  non-engineering colleague.
  ::

  ::field{name="Design / UX" type="decides how it feels"}
  Research, wireframes, visual design, and the design system you will implement
  against. If you are on frontend you will speak to design daily.
  ::

  ::field{name="Data / Analytics" type="says what happened"}
  Pipelines, warehouses, dashboards, and the numbers that decide whether the
  thing you shipped worked.
  ::

  ::field{name="Support" type="hears about it first"}
  Talks to users when something breaks. The best early-warning system in the
  company, and the team most often ignored by engineers who then debug for three
  days something Support could have explained in a sentence.
  ::

  ::field{name="Sales, HR, Finance" type="keeps the lights on"}
  Not your daily work, but they decide the deadlines, your offer letter, and
  whether the team gets another headcount.
  ::
::

## Who you will actually talk to

In a normal week as a junior engineer:

| Every day | Your team's engineers, your lead |
| --- | --- |
| Most days | Product, QA |
| Some days | Design, DevOps |
| Rarely | Data, Support, Sales |
| Once | HR, on the way in |

## The hierarchy, honestly

**Engineer → Senior engineer → Lead / Staff → Engineering manager → Director.**
The first split, somewhere around lead, is between people who keep going deeper
technically and people who move to managing. Neither is a promotion over the
other, whatever anybody tells you at the time.

Alongside that: the **architect** designs across teams, the **scrum master** runs
the process rituals, and the **product manager** owns what gets built. None of
those three is your boss. Your manager is your boss.

::tip{icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
The single highest-value habit in your first month: when you are stuck for more
than an hour, say so in the team channel with what you tried. Juniors who do this
are described as "communicative". Juniors who do not are described as "quiet, and
we are not sure what he is working on".
::

## The interview question this answers

**"Have you worked in a team before? What was your role?"**

Even if the honest answer is a college project, describe it in these terms — who
decided what to build, who built it, who checked it, and which of those you were.
That vocabulary is the thing being tested.
