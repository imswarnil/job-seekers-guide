Here they are. Every one of these is answerable from the five lessons you just
read, and you already know all of it — this page is only that knowledge lined up
so you can hear yourself say it.

## Why this matters in a job

Because a first-round screen is thirty minutes and the opening five are warm-ups
like these. Fumbling them does not fail you on the facts; it sets a tone. A crisp
answer to "what is a process?" buys you patience for the harder question four
minutes later.

Answer in two or three sentences and stop. The most common mistake is not being
wrong, it is going on.

## The six

::accordion
---
items:
  - label: What does a computer actually do?
    icon: i-lucide-message-circle-question
    content: "It takes data in, stores it, performs arithmetic and comparisons on it, and outputs data — billions of simple operations a second, with no interpretation of what you meant. Everything else, including files, windows and applications, is software built on top of that. What they are checking — whether you describe the machine as fast and literal, or as clever. Only one of those is a person who will debug well."
  - label: How many values can a byte hold?
    icon: i-lucide-message-circle-question
    content: "256 values — 0 to 255. Eight bits, two states each, and one of the 256 patterns is zero. If the byte is signed, one bit goes to the sign and the range becomes −128 to 127. What they are checking — the off-by-one at the boundary, which is the same mistake that produces most loop and array bugs. Saying '0 to 255' without hesitating is the whole answer."
  - label: What is the difference between RAM and storage?
    icon: i-lucide-message-circle-question
    content: "RAM is volatile, small and roughly a thousand times faster; storage is persistent, large and slow. A program works in RAM and must deliberately write anything it needs to keep to storage. What they are checking — whether you can explain both data loss and slowness from one distinction. Mention the speed gap; it is the sentence that separates a memorised answer from an understood one."
  - label: What is the difference between a program and a process?
    icon: i-lucide-message-circle-question
    content: "A program is the file on disk: inert instructions. A process is a running instance of it, loaded into memory and scheduled on the processor, with its own memory space, its own open files and its own identity number. One program can have many processes. What they are checking — this is the doorway to threads, isolation and concurrency. Expect the follow-up 'and a thread?' and answer honestly if you have not got there yet."
  - label: What is an operating system for?
    icon: i-lucide-message-circle-question
    content: "It runs programs and shares the processor between them, presents storage as files and folders, enforces permissions, and mediates every request to hardware. Your code never touches the disk or the network directly — it asks, through a system call, and the kernel can say no. What they are checking — whether 'permission denied' is a mystery to you or a message with a sender."
  - label: Why does software behave differently on two machines?
    icon: i-lucide-message-circle-question
    content: "Different operating system, different versions of a runtime, different permissions, a port already in use, or something as small as filename case sensitivity — macOS and Windows treat two spellings as one file, Linux does not, and the server is usually Linux. What they are checking — that you do not treat 'works on my machine' as bad luck. Naming the case-sensitivity trap specifically tends to land, because most people have been bitten by it and few can name it."

## The one you get wrong

Not from this chapter — from the habit underneath it.

::compare{caption="'Tell me about something you got wrong.' Two answers."}
  :::compare-side{label="The answer that stalls" verdict="wrong"}
  "I don't think I've had a serious bug yet." Which reads as either no real work
  or no reflection on it, and the interviewer cannot tell which.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="The answer that lands" verdict="right"}
  "I had a shortlisting rule using greater-than where I meant greater-or-equal.
  It ran fine and quietly dropped everyone on exactly the cutoff. I found it
  because the count looked twenty too low against a number I already knew."
  :::
::

Keep a note file from today. Every bug that costs you more than an hour, write
three lines: what happened, how you found it, what the fix was. By the interview
you will have thirty, and you will need one.

## Your turn

Say all six answers out loud, timed. Not read — said, with the page closed.
Anything that takes longer than thirty seconds is not an answer yet, it is
thinking, and thinking out loud is fine in a coding round and expensive in a
warm-up.
