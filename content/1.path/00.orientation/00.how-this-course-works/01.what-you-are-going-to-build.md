The first lesson is not about a computer, because I nearly quit twice and both
times it was for the same reason: I had lost sight of what any of it was for.

So before anything else, here is the deal. What you are signing up for, what you
get, and what it costs.

## Why this matters in a job

Because the reason most people fail at this is not difficulty. It is that they
learn forty disconnected things, none of which they needed yet, and arrive at an
interview with a folder of tutorials and nothing to say. Six months of that
produces a person who can follow instructions. Six months of building one real
thing produces a person who can be hired.

I have been on the bad side of that. I could not tell you, in 2024, what any of
the things I had "learned" were for.

## The promise

By the end of this course, a person who started at zero can do six things. If
something in a lesson does not move you toward one of them, it should not be
there.

## The thing you build: the University Management App

There is one project in this course. It is never restarted, never replaced, and
there is never a second one. It is called the **University Management App** —
the software a university would actually run.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="One system. Every lesson adds a piece to this same thing."}
  :::flow-step{label="The public site" icon="i-lucide-house"}
  The university's website and the enquiry form — the front door where a
  prospective student lands and asks a question.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="The admission pipeline" icon="i-lucide-inbox"}
  Every enquiry becomes a candidate with a stage: received, under review,
  shortlisted or rejected, admitted. With notes, follow-ups and conversion
  numbers at each step.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="The portals" icon="i-lucide-users"}
  A student logs in and sees their results, fees, attendance and library books. A
  lecturer marks attendance and enters marks. An administrator manages programs,
  seats and placements.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="The dashboards" icon="i-lucide-chart-column"}
  Every number that matters, drawn: the admissions funnel, fee collection over
  time, placements per department, and the students at risk of dropping out
  before anyone has noticed.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="The AI layer" icon="i-lucide-sparkles" highlight}
  A staff assistant that answers a plain-English question, shows the query it
  ran, and lets a human check it. Built last, on purpose.
  :::
::

Underneath all of that is the engine room: the console tools and the database
reporting layer you write in the first two tracks, before the web exists for you
at all.

::real-life{title="Why a university and not a to-do app" source="Every junior portfolio a hiring manager has ever skimmed"}
A university has everything real business software has: people with different
permissions, money, a pipeline with stages, records that must not be lost, dates,
attendance, reporting, and rules that change every year because a committee said
so. A to-do list has none of it. Build this and you have built the shape of most
internal software that exists — a CRM, an ERP, a hospital system, a school, a
bank's back office. The domain is a vehicle, and it is a well-chosen one.
::

## How the course moves: the chain

This is the one rule that shapes everything. **Every lesson ends on a problem,
and the next lesson opens by solving exactly that problem.** You are never handed
a tool before you are annoyed by the thing it fixes.

Read the whole course as one sentence:

::steps{level="3"}

### The machine cannot understand you

So you learn a language — Java, properly, until it is second nature.

### Your program forgets everything when it stops

So you learn SQL, and give the university a memory that survives being switched
off.

### Nobody but you can run any of it

So you learn HTML, and give the system a body a browser can render.

### It is ugly and broken on a phone

So you learn CSS. Then nothing happens when you click, so you learn JavaScript.
Then the numbers are unreadable, so you learn to draw them.

### One file has become a swamp

So you learn the toolchain and components. Then a typo ships to production, so
you learn TypeScript. Then routing and rendering are yours to hand-roll, so you
learn Next.js.

### The data still lives on your laptop

So you learn a real hosted database with logins and permissions. Then it only
runs on your machine, so you learn hosting and DNS. Then it is live but not
smart, so you learn AI.

### It is built, and now you have to explain it

So you prepare for the interview — which is where the whole thing was going.

::

## What it costs

I am not going to pretend about this.

::pros-cons
---
title: Taking this course seriously
pros:
  - One system you can send someone, and explain every line of
  - The subjects arrive in an order where each one is already needed
  - Every interview question in it comes from work you actually did
cons:
  - It is long. The Java track alone is sixteen weeks at an hour a day
  - Two chapters are genuinely hard and you will not enjoy the first week of them
  - Reading it without typing it produces nothing at all
---
::

An hour a day matters more than a big weekend. The people I know who got through
this did twenty to forty minutes on the bad days and never went a week without
opening it.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
There is no shortcut here and I am not selling one. No placement percentage, no
guaranteed package, no certificate at the end. What is at the end is a link you
own and the ability to defend it.
::
