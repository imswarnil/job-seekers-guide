## Why this matters in a job

Because the single biggest determinant of what your first two years feel like is
not the language you learn. It is which of these three kinds of company you join,
and most people find out which one they joined after they have signed.

## The three kinds

### Service companies

TCS, Infosys, Wipro, Cognizant, Accenture, and hundreds of smaller firms. They
build and maintain software **for other companies**, who pay per project or per
person per month.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Hiring | Large volume, campus drives, aptitude test then a light technical round |
| First year | Training, then a project you were assigned to, often maintenance |
| The work | Somebody else's codebase, somebody else's deadlines, real process discipline |
| Pay curve | Modest start, slow early growth, accelerates if you switch out |
| Good for | Getting in. A first job at a service company is a real first job. |

### Product companies

Google, Microsoft, Zoho, Freshworks, Zerodha, and thousands of less famous ones.
They build **their own** software and sell it to many customers.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Hiring | Smaller volume, several technical rounds, DSA and system design |
| First year | Owning a piece of a live product, on-call, code review culture |
| The work | One codebase you get to know deeply, and you see users use it |
| Pay curve | Higher start, faster growth, more variance between companies |
| Good for | Depth, ownership, and compensation — and it is harder to get in cold |

### Startups

Under fifty people, usually funded, sometimes not.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Hiring | Fast, informal, often one conversation and a take-home |
| First year | Everything. You will touch code you have no business touching. |
| The work | Enormous learning, no process, and the roadmap changes on Tuesday |
| Pay curve | Wide. Equity that may be worth nothing. |
| Good for | People who learn fastest under pressure and can afford the risk |

## Where the money comes from, and why you should care

A service company is paid for **your hours**. A product company is paid for
**the product's value**. That one difference explains nearly everything else:

- A service company counts billable utilisation, so bench time is a problem and process is strict.
- A product company counts user growth and reliability, so on-call exists and shipping something broken is worse than shipping late.

::tip{icon="i-lucide-compass"}
Ask this in any interview: *"How does this company make money, and how does my
team affect that number?"* An interviewer who cannot answer clearly has told you
something important.
::

## The honest advice

::callout{icon="i-lucide-heart-handshake" color="primary"}
If a service company is the offer you have, take it. The first offer converts
you from "candidate" to "employed engineer", and that changes every subsequent
conversation. You are not locked in — two years of real project experience makes
the product-company interview a completely different conversation. This is
covered properly in [Offer, joining and first switch](/docs/curriculum).
::

What you should not do is spend eighteen unemployed months waiting for a product
company because the internet told you service companies are beneath you. The
internet is not paying your rent.

## The interview question this answers

**"Why do you want to work here?"**

Answer it with the shape of the company, not with flattery: what they build, who
pays for it, and which part of that you want to be near. That is the answer
almost nobody gives.
