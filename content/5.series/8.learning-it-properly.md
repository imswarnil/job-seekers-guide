## The narration

Bangalore. Training, and then two or three walk-ins.

They went badly, and for the first time in my life the reason was not mysterious.
I sat in those rooms and understood something specific:

**I was not skilled.** I had a four-year degree in this and I could not do it.

That sounds like a low point. It was the opposite. Compare it with what I
believed after Kota — *I am not clever enough* — which is unfixable and was never
true. "I am not skilled" is a completely different sentence. It has a verb you can
act on.

---

So I tried to fix it by self-study. And it did not work.

I know exactly why, and this is the most practically useful thing in this episode.

::real-life{title="Why self-study failed for me" source="Bangalore, 2018"}
Self-study assumes you can tell the difference between "I don't know this yet"
and "I have misunderstood something upstream."

I could not. Every tutorial made complete sense while I was watching it and
evaporated the moment I closed the tab. I had twenty things I half-knew and no
way of finding out which one was quietly breaking the other nineteen.

You cannot revise your way to something you never learned the first time.
::

If you are three months into a self-study plan and it is not landing, please hear
this: that is not a discipline problem. You are trying to patch a foundation you
do not have. Different problem, different fix.

---

So I joined a training institute.

Java. SQL. HTML and CSS. DBMS. Object-oriented programming.

And it went in.

Six weeks in I noticed something that genuinely shocked me: I was starting to
*understand* the language. Not memorise the shape of it — understand it. The
subject I had failed to enjoy for four years was, it turned out, fine. It had
just never been taught to me in a way that connected.

Two people did that, and I am naming them because they will never turn up in a
search result and they should:

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-graduation-cap" title="Shishir Bhatt sir"}
  Taught Java in a way that made the language make sense rather than making me
  copy it. Four years of lectures had not done that. He did it in weeks.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-database" title="S. N. Dixit sir"}
  Taught SQL. I barely write SQL now. I still use how he taught me to think about
  a query, every single week, in a completely different technology.
  :::
::

---

But I need to be straight with you about something, because "join an institute"
is not the lesson.

The institute gave me the sequence and the explanation. That is genuinely what it
was worth, and it was worth about forty thousand rupees to me at the time.

What actually got me hired was four things I did myself, and every one of them is
free:

::timeline{label="What I did that the fee did not cover"}
  :::timeline-item{title="Never bunked a single class" state="done"}
  Not one. Not because I was virtuous — because I had exactly one shot and had
  already watched myself waste four years of attendance.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Made my own notes" state="done"}
  Not the handouts. Mine, in my words. If I could not write it in my own words I
  had not understood it, and that test is brutal and free.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Wrote out my own answers to interview questions" state="done"}
  Not model answers. The way I would actually say it, written down and refined,
  so the first time I said it out loud was not in the room.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Built my own example programs" state="current"}
  So when somebody asked about inheritance I had a thing I had built and could
  explain — rather than the textbook's `Animal → Dog`, which every one of the
  other thousand candidates also had.
  :::
::

That last one is the one nobody does. An interviewer has heard `Animal → Dog`
four hundred times. Bring your own example and you are, briefly, the only person
in the room.
