Written to be read by somebody who is anxious and short on time.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Where do I start?" icon="i-lucide-circle-help"}
  At the beginning: [How the IT Industry Works](/how-the-industry-works).
  Even if you have a computer science degree. It is the part that tells you what
  the roles actually are, what they pay, and what each team does all day — and it
  is the part almost nobody is taught.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="I'm not from a CS background. Can I do this?" icon="i-lucide-circle-help"}
  Yes. A large part of who this was built for is people from mechanical, civil,
  B.Sc., commerce and completely non-technical careers. Your branch decides where
  you start, not where you finish.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="Do I need to be good at maths?" icon="i-lucide-circle-help"}
  No. Not calculus, not discrete maths, not engineering maths. You need
  arithmetic, a feel for orders of magnitude, and enough logic to reason about
  conditions and loops. You will meet Big-O notation, and the path teaches the two
  hours of it that matter rather than the semester of it that does not.

  This is the single most common reason capable people wrongly rule themselves out
  of software. If a curriculum tells you otherwise, ask it which line of production
  code it is preparing you to write.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="What is deliberately left out?" icon="i-lucide-scissors"}
  | Excluded | Why |
  | --- | --- |
  | Mathematics — calculus, discrete maths, engineering maths | Not required for a software job. |
  | Aptitude and reasoning drills | Some companies test it. It is a short, separate prep — not a subject. |
  | Microprocessors, digital electronics | Real CS subjects. Not load-bearing for software employment. |
  | Certification farming | Projects prove more, at a fraction of the cost. |
  | "Learn 12 frameworks" | One language, one track, done properly. |
  | Competitive programming as a lifestyle | Useful for one kind of interview. A poor use of your first six months. |

  A lesson that says "skip this, here is why" is as valuable as one that teaches.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="Which language should I learn?" icon="i-lucide-circle-help"}
  Java, unless you have a specific reason not to. It has the most entry-level
  roles in this market and it teaches you the habits the rest of the industry
  assumes. C++, Python and TypeScript are supported alternatives. Pick one.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="How long will this take?" icon="i-lucide-circle-help"}
  Sixteen weeks if you can give it twenty-five hours a week. Forty-four if you
  have six to eight. Both are real numbers, and neither includes the job hunt
  itself, which is its own part of the path.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="Can I skip ahead?" icon="i-lucide-circle-help"}
  Yes — nothing is locked. Every subject marks the lessons that are safe to skip
  on a first pass, and says what you lose by skipping them. Skipping deliberately
  is studying. Skipping because you got lost is drifting, and the difference is
  whether somebody told you which one you were doing.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="Is it really free?" icon="i-lucide-circle-help"}
  The core path — orientation, foundations, one language, projects, job search —
  is free and stays free. If anything is charged for later it will be genuinely
  additional, like mentorship or code review. Never the map itself.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="Will you get me a job?" icon="i-lucide-circle-help"}
  No, and anyone who says otherwise is selling something. The path teaches the
  hunt as a subject: resume, search strategy, interviews, negotiation. We do not
  broker roles, take fees or make placement claims.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="I stopped for three weeks. Is it over?" icon="i-lucide-circle-help"}
  No. Open the last lesson you finished, reread it, and continue. The only
  learner who fails here is the one who stops permanently, and stopping for three
  weeks is not that.
  :::
::
