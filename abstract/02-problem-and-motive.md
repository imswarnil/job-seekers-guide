# The Problem and the Motive

## The problem, stated plainly

A graduate finishes college. The degree certificate is real. The job is not
there. And in that gap, three things happen:

### 1. Nobody told them what actually matters

College teaches a syllabus optimised for exams, not for employment. Students
memorise for marks, then discover that the interview asks them to *explain* a
deadlock, *design* a schema, *write* a working function. The subjects were
right. The way they were taught was not.

Meanwhile the same student believes maths, aptitude formulas, and every subject
on the marksheet carry equal weight. They don't. In the software industry a
small set of subjects does almost all the work: **Operating Systems, DBMS,
Computer Networks, OOP, Data Structures & Algorithms, Software Engineering, Web
Technologies, System Design.** Nobody sits them down and says so.

### 2. The market sells them the gap

Training institutes exist because the confusion is profitable. The pitch is
always the same: *"100% placement assistance."* The price is ₹50,000 to
₹1,50,000, often financed by a parent's savings or an EMI a jobless graduate
signs for.

And the content? Recorded lectures, a printed syllabus, and a trainer reading
slides — teaching material that is **already free**, and often better, on
YouTube, on official docs, on free blog series, on open courseware from
institutes far better than the one selling the course.

The institute is not selling knowledge. It is selling:

- **Sequence** — "learn this, then this."
- **Filtering** — "ignore that, it doesn't matter."
- **Accountability** — someone expecting you tomorrow.
- **Hope** — someone saying you can do it.

All four can be delivered for free. That is the entire product hypothesis.

### 3. They don't know that job search is itself a skill

This is the one nobody names. A candidate can be technically ready and still
collect rejection after rejection because:

- the resume is a template that ATS software discards,
- they apply where everyone applies, on the day everyone applies,
- they cannot tell their own story in the first five minutes of an interview,
- they treat HR rounds as a formality instead of a round they can fail,
- they never follow up, never network, never refer-hunt,
- they take the first "no" as a verdict on their worth.

Job search is a learnable, teachable, practiceable skill. It deserves a syllabus
of its own — and on this platform, it gets one.

## The motive

I was that graduate in 2017.

I did not get placed. I joined a training institute. I was rejected **33 times**
before an offer. My first job paid **₹13,000/month — ₹1.8 LPA**. I took it
anyway, because the fresher tag is a door and you only need it opened once.

What changed everything was what happened *after*. I didn't stop. I kept
learning, and I studied the job hunt itself as a subject — how to position, how
to apply, how to interview, how to negotiate. In **three months** I moved to
Accenture, onto a Salesforce track, at a salary that changed my family's
situation.

The technical knowledge got me in the room. The **art of job search** is what
got me the seat. Nobody teaches the second part, and it is worth as much as the
first.

That is the motive: to hand the next person the map I had to draw by hand, for
free, before they hand ₹80,000 to someone who won't give them one.
